\begin{abstract}
Structural classifiers that form explicit graph concepts instead of weight
matrices promise interpretable decisions, but their failure modes and the
stability of their concept-formation process are rarely examined with the
same rigor applied to accuracy benchmarks. We study both questions on a
non-backpropagation classifier that converts images into attributed
bipartite skeleton graphs, forms per-class \emph{concept attractors} by
iterative critical-point reduction, and classifies by winner-take-all
(WTA) competition over graph-similarity scores. Against a 91.13\% baseline
on the complete-contour subset of the MNIST test set, we first dissect the
dominant confusion (digit 2 predicted as 7, 111 cases) and show that it is
a \emph{representation defect, not a ranking defect}: binarization and
thinning destroy the closed head loop of the digit before any learning
step, so the complex concept for class~2 is excluded by a complexity
pre-filter in 77.5\% of cases and the surviving simplified concept loses
raw similarity in 100\% of cases, while the ranking prior contributes zero
errors. We then report six stability studies: generalization to unfiltered
MNIST test and train splits (82.2\% and 72.0\%); dependence of formation on
sample-presentation order (topology invariant for two of three probed
concepts, node counts invariant for all); dependence on sample count
(monotone envelope widening with topology freeze); reduction convergence
(topology stabilizes after 1--37 samples); and parameter compression
(a 92.5$\times$ reduction in topological elements and 51$\times$ in stored
parameter values from training samples to concepts). The results locate
the accuracy bottleneck in the image-to-graph front end rather than in
concept formation or ranking, and quantify how compact and reproducible
the learned structural concepts are.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =========================================================================

Most modern image classifiers optimize millions of weights by
backpropagation and explain their decisions only post hoc. An alternative
line of work encodes images as attributed graphs and learns explicit
structural \emph{concepts} whose nodes, edges, and parameter ranges remain
human-readable~\cite{parzhyn2013principles,parzhyn2025architecture,lapin2025fewshot}.
In our system, an image is skeletonized into a bipartite graph of critical
points and connecting segments, per-class concepts are formed by iterative
graph reduction without gradient descent, and classification is a
winner-take-all (WTA) competition between concepts scored by an
attribute-weighted graph-similarity measure derived from graph edit
distance (GED)~\cite{bunke1983inexact,serratosa2015}. The approach reached
91.13\% accuracy on the complete-contour subset of the MNIST test
set~\cite{lecun1998gradient} with only 13 concepts formed from 875
training samples --- two orders of magnitude fewer parameters than a
comparable neural model, and every parameter inspectable.

Accuracy alone, however, says little about \emph{why} such a system fails
when it fails, and whether the concepts it forms are stable artifacts or
accidents of training order and sample choice. Both questions matter more
for structural classifiers than for weight-based ones precisely because
the internal representation claims semantic meaning: a concept that
changes with presentation order, or an error whose cause cannot be located
in the pipeline, would undermine the interpretability argument itself.

This paper answers both questions empirically. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{An anatomy of the dominant error.} The most frequent
confusion of the baseline (true digit 2 predicted as 7; 111 of 792 errors)
superficially contradicts the WTA design: the concept for class~2 is
\emph{more} complex than the one for class~7, so it should win when it
matches. We trace all 111 cases through the production scoring code and
show a two-stage mechanism with a single root cause --- anchor-point loss
during image-to-graph construction, \emph{before} any reduction or
learning step (Section~\ref{sec:postmortem}).
\item \textbf{Six stability studies of concept formation}, covering
generalization to other MNIST splits, sample-order dependence, sample-count
dependence, augmentation, reduction convergence, and parameter compression
(Section~\ref{sec:studies}).
\item \textbf{A diagnosis with design consequences}: errors originate in
the representation front end and cannot be repaired downstream, while the
formation process itself is largely order-invariant, converges after few
samples, and compresses input structure by roughly two orders of magnitude
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

All analyses run on the production code paths (no re-implemented scoring):
the post-mortem tool reproduces the live classifier's prediction for
111 of 111 studied images.

\section{System Overview}
\label{sec:system}
% =========================================================================

We summarize the pipeline only to the depth needed for the analyses; the
representation and formation method are described
in~\cite{lapin2025fewshot,parzhyn2025energy}.

\subsection{Image-to-graph construction}

An input image is binarized (intensity threshold 110--180, adaptively
retried on disconnection), thinned to a one-pixel
skeleton~\cite{zhang1984fast}, and approximated by a Growing Neural Gas
network~\cite{fritzke1995growing} whose output polyline is simplified with
the Ramer--Douglas--Peucker algorithm~\cite{douglas1973algorithms}. The
result is an attributed \emph{bipartite} graph: \emph{Point} nodes
(endpoints, corners, junctions, a start point) alternate with \emph{Vector}
nodes (line segments carrying length, direction, and quadrant attributes).
Node coordinates are normalized to a centered $[-1,1]$ system for scale
and translation invariance. We define the \emph{complexity} of a graph as
its total number of nodes plus edges.

\subsection{Concept formation}

For each class, training graphs are merged one at a time into a concept by
critical-point reduction: shared topology is retained, instance-specific
branches are reduced, and each surviving node accumulates per-feature
\emph{ranges} (envelopes) instead of point values. The first sample acts
as the seed of the concept; formation is incremental and uses no gradient
signal. A class may yield more than one concept (e.g.\ two stylistic
variants of the digit); the baseline uses 13 concepts for 10 classes,
formed from 875 pre-augmented training samples.

\subsection{Classification}

A test graph is compared against all concepts that survive a
\emph{complexity pre-filter}: concepts with complexity greater than the
image complexity are excluded before any comparison, on the rationale that
a simple image cannot instantiate a more complex concept. Surviving
concepts are scored by a GED-based similarity over 13 node
features with graduated substitution costs and per-node diagnostic weights
(inverse to the width of the concept's learned feature range,
$\varepsilon = 0.1$). The final ranking applies a complexity prior
($\lambda = 0.10$) that rewards more complex concepts, implementing the
WTA principle that a rich concept should beat a poor one when both match.
The winner's class is the prediction.

\subsection{Baseline}
\label{sec:baseline}

The reference run\footnote{Run \texttt{run\_20260630\_235356}; artifacts
(configuration, metrics, confusion matrix, per-image errors, concept
graphs) are archived with the code.} evaluates the full \emph{complete-only}
MNIST test subset: 8{,}704 of the 10{,}000 test images whose contours are
complete (unbroken strokes), a restriction discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:datasets}. Of 8{,}707 submitted images\footnote{Three
images were resubmitted after transient pipeline errors.}, 22 failed in
skeletonization (dead-letter queue) and 8{,}685 were classified, with
accuracy $91.13\%$, precision $91.92\%$, recall $91.13\%$, and F1
$91.34\%$ over the classified set. Counting pipeline failures as errors
yields $90.89\%$ over all submitted images.
Figure~\ref{fig:confusion} shows the confusion structure. The largest
confusion pairs are $2{\to}7$ (111), $4{\to}7$ (62), $2{\to}6$ (57),
$4{\to}1$ (52), and $9{\to}4$ (46); classes 2 (215 errors) and 4 (155
errors) dominate the error mass.

\section{Anatomy of the Dominant Error: Why 2s Fall into 7s}
\label{sec:postmortem}
% =========================================================================

The $2{\to}7$ confusion is theoretically interesting because it seems to
contradict the WTA design. The full concept for class~2 (\cid{2\_2},
complexity 24) is far richer than the concept for class~7 (\cid{7\_1},
complexity 9, the smallest in the system). Under WTA with a complexity
prior, the richer concept should win whenever it genuinely matches. The
supervisor's hypothesis was that \cid{2\_2} never gets to compete: the
image graph loses critical anchor points during construction --- before
reduction --- so the ``2'' arrives at the classifier already impoverished.

We verified this hypothesis on all 111 misclassified images using the
production \texttt{ClassificationOrchestrator} and cost functions; the
offline tool reproduced the live prediction for 111/111 images. The
mechanism is two-staged, and both stages share one root cause.

\subsection{Stage 1: the complexity pre-filter excludes the rich concept}

The median complexity of the 111 misclassified ``2'' graphs is 21
(range 13--29), below the complexity 24 of \cid{2\_2}. Consequently the
pre-filter removes \cid{2\_2} from the competition in \textbf{86 of 111
cases (77.5\%)}. The rich concept does not \emph{lose} the WTA
competition --- it never enters it.

\subsection{Stage 2: the simplified concept loses on raw similarity}

The simplified concept \cid{2\_1} (complexity 13) passes the filter but
systematically loses to \cid{7\_1} on \emph{raw} similarity, with a mean
gap of 0.14 (0.09 in the subgroup where \cid{2\_2} also competed).
Bucket analysis over all 111 cases (Table~\ref{tab:buckets}) shows that
\textbf{every single error is a raw-similarity loss} (bucket B); there are
no cases where no 2-concept competed at all (bucket A) and --- crucially ---
no cases where a 2-concept won on raw similarity but lost the final
$\lambda$-ranking (bucket C). The ranking prior is innocent. Notably, in
the 25 cases where \cid{2\_2} did compete, it never outscored \cid{2\_1}:
the impoverished image graph is genuinely closer to the simplified
topology.

Figure~\ref{fig:construction} traces a canonical exemplar
(\texttt{mnist\_test\_2\_00766}, a ``2'' with a clearly closed head loop)

resulting graph next to the competing concepts.

The head of the ``2'' is a closed loop in the original image. After
binarization and thinning, the loop becomes an \emph{open hook}: the
skeleton has zero cycles, and the loop-closure critical point is gone
before GNG even runs. GNG ($\approx$35 neurons) with RDP simplification
then flattens the hook arc into two straight segments, leaving a
zigzag-like graph of 6 structural points (3 endpoints, 1 junction, 0
cycles). The final bipartite graph has 11 nodes and 10 edges ---
complexity 21 $<$ 24, so \cid{2\_2} is filtered out. The production
ranking for this exemplar is shown in Table~\ref{tab:exemplar}.

The per-feature breakdown of the \cid{2\_1} comparison shows \emph{where}
the loss occurs: surviving points sit outside the spatial ranges the
concept learned to expect --- e.g.\ \texttt{distance\_to\_centroid} $=
0.558 \notin [0.69, 1.00]$ on the start point and \texttt{normalized\_x}
$= 0.200 \notin [-0.70, 0.10]$ on a corner point. The geometry of the
graph is distorted by the loss of the loop, so even the simplified concept
matches poorly.

Not every case is a skeletonization victim: a contrasting exemplar
(\texttt{mnist\_test\_2\_00984}, Figure~\ref{fig:contrast}) is a flat
cursive ``2'' visually close to a ``7'', where the miss is largely honest
stylistic ambiguity. But the median image complexity of 21 against
\cid{2\_2}'s 24 identifies graph impoverishment as the dominant factor
across the 111 cases.

\subsection{Consequences}

Three design consequences follow, none of which requires hard-coding
concept identities. (i)~\emph{Skeletonization is the primary lever}:
preserving the head loop (cycle-preserving thinning or closing, or
loop-aware pruning) would raise image complexity, return \cid{2\_2} to the
competition, and let its cycle-count features discriminate against
\cid{7\_1}. (ii)~The pre-filter can be softened \emph{as a property}
(a tolerance $\delta$ proportional to the width of the concept's learned
ranges) rather than as a constant, but this treats the symptom: without
the loop, \cid{2\_2} still loses. (iii)~The $\lambda$-ranking prior should
\emph{not} be touched --- bucket C is empty.

\section{Stability Studies of Concept Formation}
\label{sec:studies}
% =========================================================================

The supervisor's second question concerns the trustworthiness of the
formation process itself: does the classifier generalize beyond the
curated test subset, and are the concepts it forms stable with respect to
presentation order, sample count, augmentation, and reduction depth? Six
studies address this. All formation studies run the production formation
code through a seedable offline probe; all evaluation studies run the full
live pipeline.

\subsection{Generalization across MNIST dataset variants}
\label{sec:datasets}

The system is contour-based: a digit drawn with broken strokes yields an
incomplete skeleton that no concept formed from complete contours can
cover. The baseline therefore evaluates the complete-contour subset. To
quantify the cost of this restriction, we evaluated the untouched baseline
concepts on the \emph{full} MNIST test set (10{,}000 images, no
exclusions) and on the 60{,}000-image training split, whose
completeness labels come from a heuristic
classifier\footnote{The 60k split is labeled by a completeness heuristic,
not manual review; $\approx$875 of its images are the system's own
training samples (leakage $\leq 1.5\%$).}. Table~\ref{tab:datasets}
reports the results.

Three observations. First, the completeness restriction is load-bearing:
incomplete contours in the test set score 23.94\%, dragging the
no-exclusions accuracy to 82.21\%. Second, the drop on the 60k
\emph{complete} split (76.12\% vs.\ 90.89\%) indicates that heuristic
completeness labels are noisier than manual review --- part of the
``complete'' 60k split is likely not. Third, accuracy over all 70{,}000
MNIST images with 13 concepts and no per-split tuning is 73.42\%, which
bounds the current representation's coverage from below.

\subsection{Dependence on sample-presentation order}
\label{sec:order}

Formation is incremental and the first sample seeds the concept, so order
dependence is a structural risk. (Historically, the production order from
the graph store was nondeterministic; it is now fixed to a deterministic
ordering.) We re-formed three representative concepts --- \cid{2\_2} (rich,
12 nodes), \cid{7\_1} (minimal, 5 nodes), \cid{8\_1} (cyclic, 11 nodes) ---
under 10 random presentation orders each (Table~\ref{tab:order}).

No formation failures occurred. Node counts are \emph{identical across all
orders for all three concepts}. \cid{7\_1} and \cid{8\_1} converge to
byte-identical topologies regardless of order; \cid{2\_2} reaches an
identical topology in 64\% of order pairs with a mean pairwise GED of
0.36 --- small residual variation concentrated in the richest concept.
Parameter-envelope widths vary mildly with order (e.g.\ \cid{2\_2}:
0.438--0.492). Formation is thus largely, though not perfectly,
order-invariant; the deterministic-ordering fix makes production retrains
reproducible by construction.

\subsection{Dependence on sample count}
\label{sec:samplecount}

We formed each of the 13 concepts from $n \in \{2, 5, 10, 20\}$ samples
(three random draws per condition). Two monotone trends emerge
(Figure~\ref{fig:curves}): node counts \emph{decrease or stay constant}
as $n$ grows (topology is pruned toward the shared core, then freezes ---
e.g.\ \cid{2\_2}: $16 \to 12.7 \to 12 \to 12$ nodes), while mean
range widths \emph{increase} monotonically (the envelope absorbs
instance variation --- e.g.\ \cid{7\_1}: $0.33 \to 0.48 \to 0.68 \to 0.80$).
More samples buy generality, not structure: structure is set early and
additional samples widen tolerances.

The study also exposed a formation fragility: concept \cid{4\_1} fails to
form at higher sample counts (67\% of draws crash at $n{=}5$, 100\% at
$n \geq 10$), showing that for topologically heterogeneous classes the
reduction can fail outright rather than degrade gracefully.

% =========================================================================
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =========================================================================

\subsection{The bottleneck is the representation front end}

The error anatomy and the stability studies point at the same conclusion
from two directions. The dominant confusion is fully explained by
anchor-point loss during binarization, thinning, and polyline
simplification --- upstream of concept formation, matching, and ranking,
each of which behaves exactly as designed given its input. Meanwhile the
formation process itself is reproducible (order study), converges after
few samples (convergence study), and compresses inputs by two orders of
magnitude while preserving the property inventory (compression study).
Reduction cannot restore what construction destroyed: no downstream
parameter --- pre-filter tolerance, cost ladder, ranking prior --- can
recover a cycle that no longer exists in the skeleton. The productive
repair is cycle-preserving skeletonization, which would restore image
complexity and re-admit rich concepts into the WTA competition.

\subsection{Concepts behave like attractors}

The studies substantiate the term \emph{concept attractor} used in prior
work~\cite{lapin2025fewshot}: identical node counts under all
presentation orders, topology freeze after a class-dependent but small
number of samples, and monotone envelope widening thereafter are exactly
the signature of a fixed point of the reduction process, approached from
different initial conditions. The exception, \cid{4\_1}, marks the limit
of the current reduction operators on topologically heterogeneous classes
--- it fails loudly (formation crash) rather than silently, which is the
preferable failure mode for an interpretable system.

\subsection{Limitations}

The 60k-split completeness labels are heuristic, so the 76.12\%
``complete'' figure conflates model error with label noise;
$\approx$1.5\% of that split are the system's own training images. The
order and convergence studies probe three and thirteen concepts of one
dataset, respectively; MNIST's structural diversity is limited. The
augmentation study and per-sample-count downstream accuracy await
destructive retrain runs. \todo{Update once Tier~C results land.}
Finally, the complete-contour restriction is itself a limitation of the
contour-based representation, quantified in
Table~\ref{tab:datasets} rather than hidden by it.

% =========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =========================================================================

We dissected the dominant error of a graph-based non-backpropagation
classifier and measured the stability of its concept-formation process.
The headline confusion ($2 {\to} 7$) is not a failure of winner-take-all
competition: the richer concept is excluded by the complexity pre-filter
in 77.5\% of cases and the remaining losses are raw-similarity losses
caused by the same input defect --- destruction of the digit's head loop
during binarization and thinning. The ranking prior contributes zero
errors. Concept formation itself proved order-invariant in node counts,
convergent after 1--37 samples depending on class richness, and highly
compressive (92.5$\times$ in topology, 51$\times$ in stored values),
while generalization tests bound uncurated-MNIST accuracy at 82.21\% (test
split) and 73.42\% (all 70{,}000 images).

Future work follows directly: cycle-preserving skeletonization as the
primary accuracy lever; property-based softening of the complexity
pre-filter; hardened reduction operators for topologically heterogeneous
classes; and completion of the augmentation and sample-count retrain arms.
